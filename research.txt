Hubble and Humason were specifically looking for the Calcium H and K lines. On Earth, or in our own sun, ionized calcium always absorbs light at exact, known wavelengths (around 393.4 and 396.8 nanometers, in the violet part of the spectrum). It acts like an undeniable chemical barcode.

When Humason developed the glass plates of distant galaxies, the "barcode" was there, but it was in the wrong place. The Calcium H and K lines had been shifted toward the red end of the spectrum.
To calculate the redshift (z), they used a straightforward formula comparing the observed wavelength on the glass plate (λobs​) to the known, resting wavelength measured in a laboratory on Earth (λrest​):
z=λrest​λobs​−λrest​​
Because the galaxies Hubble was observing were relatively "close" in cosmological terms, their recession velocities were a small fraction of the speed of light. Therefore, to translate this redshift into a physical velocity (v), they could use the simple non-relativistic Doppler formula, multiplying the redshift by the speed of light (c):
v=c×z
Using this math, Humason calculated that some of these galaxies were racing away from Earth at staggering speeds—up to thousands of kilometers per second.
The Graph That Broke the Static Universe
Velocities alone were not enough to prove the universe was expanding. If the universe were just a chaotic swarm, some galaxies would be moving toward us (blueshift) and some away (redshift) randomly.
The knockout punch came when Hubble combined Humason's velocity calculations with his own distance measurements. Hubble had spent years calculating the distance to these galaxies by observing Cepheid variables—pulsating stars whose absolute brightness is tied to their pulse rate, allowing them to be used as "standard candles" for measuring cosmic yardage.
Hubble plotted Humason's calculated velocities on the Y-axis against his own calculated distances on the X-axis.
The result was a stunningly clear upward-sloping line: v=H0​×d (Velocity equals the Hubble constant times distance).
The further away a galaxy was, the faster it was receding. The only physical, geometric explanation for this precise correlation was that the fabric of the universe itself was stretching uniformly in all directions. When Einstein saw this linear relationship, derived directly from those faint, hard-won glass plates, the mathematical debate was over.
