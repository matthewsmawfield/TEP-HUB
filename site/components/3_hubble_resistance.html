2. Hubble's Resistance and the Hijacked Data

The standard historical narrative asserts that Edwin Hubble discovered the expanding universe. A forensic examination of the era's primary literature reveals the precise opposite: Hubble actively resisted the kinematic interpretation of his own data until the end of his life, repeatedly warning that attributing redshift to spatial expansion resulted in physical absurdities. The data was effectively hijacked by theorists determined to stretch the universe to fit the Lemaître-Tolman metric.

2.1 Apparent Velocities and the Ocean Pressure Analogy

In his seminal 1929 paper, Hubble obsessively enclosed the word "velocity" in quotation marks. He understood that the linear relation $v = H_0 d$ was merely an empirical mapping of redshift to distance, not a proof of physical motion. To utilize an oceanographic analogy, Hubble provided a map showing that pressure increases linearly with depth. Instead of deducing a continuous gradient within a stable medium, the theorists of the 1930s concluded that the water molecules themselves must be accelerating away from the observer.

Fritz Zwicky recognized this fallacy immediately. In 1929, he proposed his "Tired Light" hypothesis, arguing that redshift was a signature of energy loss traversing vast cosmic distances, famously dismissing the expanding-universe theorists as succumbing to a herd mentality. Walther Nernst similarly argued for an eternal, thermodynamic continuum, insisting that the universe must exist in a state of stable equilibrium. While Zwicky's specific scattering mechanism was flawed, his fundamental instinct—that redshift is a property of the intervening medium rather than a kinematic Doppler shift—was physically sound.

2.2 The 1935 Surface Brightness Failure

Hubble's skepticism culminated in a direct empirical challenge. Working with Richard Tolman in 1935, Hubble designed a definitive test: if the universe is expanding, the surface brightness of distant galaxies must rapidly diminish due to the combined effects of the inverse-square law, the reduction in photon arrival rates, and the energy loss per photon. If the universe is static, brightness should diminish far less severely.

The observational data heavily favored a static universe. Rather than abandoning the expanding metric, theorists invented arbitrary "evolutionary corrections"—asserting that early galaxies were simply much brighter than modern ones, perfectly offsetting the expected dimming. Hubble viewed this as theoretical tailoring, stretching the man to fit the suit. By 1937, he issued a final ultimatum, noting that an expanding universe leads to an inexplicably, suspiciously young cosmos, and pleaded that the redshift must be due to "some hitherto unrecognized principle of nature."
